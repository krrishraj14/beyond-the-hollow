# Beyond the Hollow — Story & Design Doc (Draft v1)

*Title locked in: **Beyond the Hollow**.*

## Premise

Humanity has pushed out from a dying homeworld to a distant, mostly unmapped star cluster. **You are the commander of a small expedition crew** whose ship goes down on a hostile frontier world — no easy resupply, no backup. You have to survive, explore, fight, and **build a real foothold** on not one but **two connected planets**, while uncovering why an ancient alien presence seems tied to your ship's crash in the first place.

**Tone:** Epic and heroic, not grimdark. Stakes are real and losses matter, but the throughline is hope — a found family building something against the odds.

## The Two Planets

Both are meant to be **deeply explorable**, not just a hub-and-a-side-area. Draft split:

### Planet A — "Kessa's Reach" (your first foothold)
- Temperate, overgrown ruins-and-wilderness world. This is where you crash and where your first colony takes root.
- Mix of dense alien forest, crumbling pre-human ruins, and open plains good for early base placement.
- Home to Danger Type 1 (hostile wildlife) most heavily, with pockets of rival faction presence.

### Planet B — "The Hollow Verge" — a massive alien megastructure
- Not a natural planet in the usual sense — a colossal ancient alien construct (a dyson-swarm-like shell, a ring-world, or a structure built *around/into* a planetary body). Bigger in scope and more to explore than Planet A: multiple interior zones/levels, alien tech, and the deepest story revelations.
- This is where the **ancient alien threat** truly lives, and where Dr. Osei's arc pays off.
- **Gated by player performance, not just time or resources.** Access to the megastructure isn't automatic — only players who've proven themselves on Planet A (survived its toughest threats, completed key story/skill milestones, maybe hit a colony-strength or crew-skill threshold) earn the way in. This makes Planet A feel like a real proving ground rather than a tutorial, and gives strong players something to strive for.
  - *(To design later: what exactly counts as "performing well" — could be a combination of combat proficiency, exploration completion %, colony development level, and key story triggers, rather than one single stat.)*

*(Open design question: is the "way in" a literal gate/ship/portal you unlock, framed narratively as needing to prove yourselves to something watching? That would tie the performance-gating directly into the story rather than feeling like a game-y difficulty wall.)*

## The Crew (Companions)

Locked to **two** named companions — tight, focused cast:

| Name (placeholder) | Role | Personality hook | Story tie-in |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reyes** | Pilot / mechanic | Blunt, loyal, secretly terrified you'll all die out here but won't admit it | Knows something about why the ship's course changed before the crash |
| **Dr. Amara Osei** | Scientist / xenobiologist | Endlessly curious, pushes you toward the ruins even when it's risky | The one who first identifies the alien presence is *not* dormant |

This pairing gives a nice split: **Reyes** anchors the "keep us alive, keep the ship/colony running" side, and **Osei** pulls you toward the mystery and the ruins. Between expeditions, expect banter/friction between "we should fortify" (Reyes) vs. "we should investigate" (Osei) — a good source of dialogue and player-facing tension.

## Core Gameplay Loop

1. **From the colony**, plan an expedition (choose crew member(s) to bring, gear up)
2. **Explore** — ruins, wilderness, alien structures — fight threats, find resources/story items
3. **Return** — resources fund colony growth (housing, defenses, research); story items unlock dialogue/plot
4. **Manage** the colony between expeditions — assign crew roles, handle colonist needs, defend against occasional raids/incursions
5. Repeat, with **stakes and threats escalating**, eventually unlocking travel to Planet B

## Danger Types (all three, layered in)

- **Hostile wildlife** — the "wild frontier" danger, mostly Planet A, ambient threat
- **Rival human faction** — not purely hostile. A faction competing for the same frontier resources, but containing individuals worth negotiating with — some may be recruitable, become informants, or offer trades/truces, while others remain genuinely dangerous. This gives the player real choices (fight vs. talk) rather than a faceless enemy type.
- **Ancient alien threat** — the mystery/plot danger, mostly Planet B, ties to the crash and to Dr. Osei's discoveries

## Decided So Far

- ✅ Crew: **Reyes** (pilot/mechanic) + **Dr. Amara Osei** (scientist) — just the two
- ✅ Planet B is a **megastructure**, larger/deeper to explore than Planet A, and **gated by player performance** on Planet A
- ✅ Rival human faction is **not purely hostile** — some members are worth negotiating with
- ✅ **Branching dialogue with real consequences** — player choices shape relationships and story outcomes, not just flavor text
- ✅ Access to Planet B is measured by **a mix of combat/survival, exploration completion, and colony strength** — no single stat gates it
- ✅ The gating itself is **narrative, not just mechanical** — something in/around the megastructure is watching and testing the player, and "unlocking" it is a story beat, not a silent difficulty wall
- ✅ Story decisions: **a handful of big, impactful branches**, not many small ones
- ✅ Reyes/Osei relationship is **felt through dialogue and behavior only** — no visible meter/stat
- ✅ The watching presence is named **Ashketh**

## The Watching Presence — "Ashketh" (working name)

An ancient AI, the last active custodian of a long-vanished precursor civilization — **the Architects** — who built the megastructure and ultimately destroyed themselves through their own unchecked power. Since then, Ashketh's purpose has been to test every newcomer who reaches its threshold, not for raw strength, but for **restraint, cooperation, and judgment** — the qualities the Architects lacked at the end.

**Story functions this serves:**
- Explains the crash: Ashketh (or something acting on its behalf) subtly influenced the ship's course — it doesn't want random arrivals, it wants to observe how the crew handles adversity before they even know they're being watched.
- Gives **Dr. Osei** a real arc: she's the one who starts detecting patterns/signals that don't behave like natural phenomena — the bridge between "surviving a hostile world" and "someone built this and is watching."
- Justifies the **mixed-metric gate** into Planet B — Ashketh isn't grading one skill, it's grading whether the player deserves what the Architects couldn't handle.
- Keeps the **heroic, hopeful tone** — Ashketh isn't cruel, and may even offer cryptic help if the player is doing well. It wants them to pass.

*(Locked in: **Ashketh**.)*

## Big Decision Points (locked: all four)

**1. The Rival Faction — Fight or Fold Them In**
Trigger: the crew encounters the rival faction **while both sides are under attack by hostile wildlife** — a shared moment of danger that forces an immediate call. Player chooses: fight past them and treat them purely as competition, or extend a truce in the heat of the moment. Consequences ripple forward — a stronger colony (more hands, potential allies/companions later) vs. a "purer" colony fully under the player's control (but a lasting rival threat).

**2. The Crash — Confront What Reyes Knows**
Reyes has been hiding something about why the ship's course changed right before the crash. Player chooses: push for an immediate confession (risks trust, gets answers early) or let Reyes reveal it in their own time (builds trust, but delays key information). Relationship-defining choice for Reyes specifically.

**3. Osei's Discovery — Share It or Suppress It**
Osei finds hard evidence the crash wasn't an accident and something is watching the colony. Player chooses: tell the whole colony (morale risk, but builds trust/unity with colonists) or keep it contained to the crew (avoids panic, isolates the player and deepens the crew's private burden). Shapes colonist reactions later when Ashketh's presence becomes undeniable.

**4. The Threshold — Answering Ashketh's Test**
Right at the Planet B gate, the player faces Ashketh's test directly — how they answer it (values shown: strength, restraint, cooperation, curiosity, etc.) shapes the tone and outcome of the ending.

## Ashketh's Presence on Planet A

Locked to **fleeting voice/vision moments**: once or twice, a crew member (likely Osei, given her sensitivity to the anomalies) experiences a brief, wordless presence near a major ruin — a pressure, a held breath, a half-heard tone — never explained until Planet B. Keeps the foreshadowing minimal and eerie rather than over-signposted, and gives Osei's arc a personal, unsettling throughline before the big reveal.

## Design Implications Worth Flagging

- **Dialogue system** should track a small number of major branch points/flags rather than a granular stat system — Reyes/Osei reactions shift based on these flags and on smaller in-the-moment choices, surfaced only through their tone/dialogue, never a UI meter
- The "mix of everything" unlock condition for Planet B means we'll want a simple way to track player progress across combat, exploration, and colony stats — doesn't need to be complex, but should be visible to the player (e.g. a "Readiness" summary) so it doesn't feel like a hidden black box
- Osei's vision moments should be rare and understated (once or twice on Planet A) so the Planet B reveal still lands as a payoff, not a repeat

## Planet B — "The Hollow Verge": Full Design

### The Structure
A **ring-world / orbital megastructure**, built by the Architects and reached by ship — not a natural planet. Larger and deeper to explore than Planet A, with multiple distinct interior zones/levels. Its systems (once functional guardian tech, environmental controls, etc.) are now decaying and corrupted after the Architects' fall, which is part of what makes it dangerous.

### The Spaceship — a mid-game reward, not a starting tool

**Origin**: built partly new, partly salvaged — Reyes uses the crashed ship hull (the same wreck already sitting near the colony) as a base, fabricating the rest from colony resources. It's *your* ship, rebuilt from the thing that stranded you — a nice narrative full-circle.

**Trigger**: unlocks purely on a **Readiness threshold (50%)** — no separate resource cost. Once the player crosses that line, Reyes announces he can get the wreck flying again.

**Flight model**: **full free 6-directional flight** — pitch, roll, and yaw, not an arcade up/down glide. This is a real flight-sim-style control scheme, third-person chase camera behind the ship.

**Boarding**: the ship is parked at the crash site once repaired. Walk up, interact to board; must land to exit (no mid-air bailout for now).

**Combat**: the ship is **transport + a weapon** — a forward-facing ship-mounted gun the player can fire while flying.
- **Now (Planet A)**: strafes existing ground enemies/rivals from the air.
- **Later**: introduces **aerial scout drones** as a new threat — small, fast Architect-adjacent probes that foreshadow the Guardian constructs waiting on Planet B. Not required for the first build pass.

**Fuel**: **limited energy that recharges** — trickles back slowly during flight, and refills quickly/fully when docked back at the colony beacon. This gives flight real limits without being punishing, and gives the beacon a second purpose (refuel point).

### Getting to Planet B
Not player-initiated by choice or menu — **Ashketh actively pulls the player in once it decides they're ready** (tied to the Readiness threshold). This keeps the gating narrative rather than mechanical: the player doesn't "unlock" Planet B so much as get *summoned* to it, reinforcing that Ashketh has been watching and choosing the moment itself.

### Threats on Planet B — two flavors, mirroring Planet A's dual-danger structure
- **Guardian constructs** — corrupted/malfunctioning remnants of Architect defense tech. Disciplined, mechanical, systemic in behavior (patrols, turrets, sentries) rather than feral.
- **Mutated creatures** — failed experiments left behind by the Architects; feral and chaotic, a twisted echo of Kessa's Reach's wildlife.
- Together these reinforce the theme: the Architects' hubris broke both their machines and their living experiments. Planet A had wildlife + a rival faction; Planet B has guardians + mutants — same two-threat shape, recontextualized.

### Base-building on Planet B, adapted
Not a lush colony like Kessa's Reach — think **footholds, waypoints, and repair stations** rather than settling. The framing is "securing ground in hostile territory," not "building a home." Mechanically similar system to Planet A's colony building, but re-skinned and likely smaller in scale per-location since the structure itself is the antagonist.

### The Threshold — Decision #4, played out as a sequence
Rather than a single dialogue choice, the Threshold is **a sequence of escalating trial chambers**, each testing a specific value Ashketh cares about — strength, restraint, cooperation, and potentially others (judgment, curiosity). This is the climactic gameplay sequence of the whole game.
- Earlier choices should echo here: how the player handled Reyes' confession, Osei's discovery, and the rival faction could subtly influence how these trials play out, or how Ashketh reacts/comments during them — paying off the "choices shape the story" promise at the highest-stakes moment.
- How the player approaches each trial (not just pass/fail) shapes the tone and outcome of the ending.

### Threshold — Final Details (locked in)
- **3–4 trial chambers**, each testing one specific value (strength, restraint, cooperation, and a possible 4th — judgment or curiosity)
- **Failing a trial lets the player retry** — no game over, no permanent lockout. Keeps the climax tense but fair, consistent with the heroic (not punishing) tone
- **One ending**, but its **tone and details shift based on the choices made throughout the game** — how Reyes' confession was handled, whether Osei's discovery was shared or suppressed, and how the rival faction encounter resolved should all color how the ending actually reads, even though the broad outcome is singular. This keeps scope manageable while still making every earlier decision feel like it mattered.
